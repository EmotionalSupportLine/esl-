"I don't want to burden my friends. They've heard this story a hundred times. They have their own stuff going on."

So you go quiet. You say "I'm fine" when you're not. You scroll instead of texting. And the thing you need to talk about just sits in your chest, getting heavier.

Let's talk about that.

## Why do I feel like I'm a burden?

A few common reasons:

- **You're the strong one.** You're the friend everyone calls. Being on the other side feels strange, maybe even wrong.
- **You've been leaning for a while.** A divorce, a sick parent, a job that's grinding you down. When it goes on for months, you start to feel like a broken record.
- **You learned it young.** Some of us grew up in homes where needing things caused trouble. So we learned not to need things.
- **Your friends really are stretched.** Sometimes it's not in your head. Everyone is tired, and you can feel it.

None of these mean you should handle everything alone. They just mean you might need more than one place to put it.

## Is it wrong to lean on friends?

No. Leaning on each other is what friendship is for. Most people feel closer to someone who trusts them with something real.

But it helps to lean well. A friend is a wonderful listener and a terrible full-time support system. Nobody can be that for anyone, and when we try, both people get worn out. The answer isn't to stop telling your friends anything. It's to spread the weight around.

## How can I ask a friend for support without feeling guilty?

Try being specific. Vague requests ("Can we talk?") make people brace for something huge. Clear requests are easier to say yes to.

Here are a few scripts you can borrow:

- "Do you have 15 minutes to just listen? I don't need advice, I just need to say this out loud."
- "I'm having a rough day. Could you send me something funny?"
- "Can I vent for five minutes, and then I want to hear about you?"
- "I don't need you to fix it. I just don't want to sit with it alone."

And give your friends room to say "not right now." A no today isn't a no forever.

If even thinking about asking makes your chest tighten, start with your body first. Here's how: [nervous system regulation in real life](/blog/nervous-system-regulation-in-real-life).

## Who can I talk to besides my friends?

This is the real question. Here are some options:

- **A support group** for the specific thing you're carrying. Other people in it get it without needing the backstory.
- **Your doctor or a licensed therapist,** if this has been heavy for a long time, or is affecting your sleep, eating, work or health.
- **A coach,** if what you need is a plan and someone to hold you to it.
- **Someone neutral who is paid to listen.** That's what we do.

## What's the difference between talking to a friend and talking to a supporter?

A friend knows your history, loves you and has opinions about your ex. That's a gift. It can also make some things hard to say.

A trained supporter has no stake in your choices. We don't need you to stay or leave, forgive or not forgive. That neutral space can make it easier to hear yourself think. And because there's no relationship to protect, there's no guilt about taking up time. The time is already yours.

## A quick exercise: map your support

Grab a piece of paper and make four boxes:

1. **Who I can laugh with.**
2. **Who I can cry with.**
3. **Who I can ask for practical help** (a ride, a meal, a hand).
4. **Who I can think out loud with.**

Write names in each box. Be honest. If one name shows up in every box, that person is carrying a lot, and you might be too. If a box is empty, that's useful to know. It tells you where to look for more support.
