Every August, Earth passes through a trail of dust left behind by a comet called Swift-Tuttle. The tiny particles burn up in our atmosphere as shooting stars. That's the **Perseid meteor shower**, one of the most loved sky events of the year.

In 2027, the Perseids are expected to peak on the night of **August 12 into the early hours of August 13**.

## The moon this year

The moon matters a lot for meteor watching, because bright moonlight washes out fainter meteors. On the peak night the moon is a bright waxing gibbous, but it sets after midnight. That leaves the dark hours before dawn free of moonlight, which is the best window for watching anyway.

## How to watch the Perseids

**Go somewhere dark.** The farther from city lights, the more you'll see.

**Watch after midnight.** Meteor numbers usually increase in the hours before dawn.

**Get comfortable.** Bring a reclining chair or a blanket and lie back. You'll be looking up for a while.

**Give your eyes time.** It takes about 20 to 30 minutes for your eyes to adjust to the dark. Put your phone away, since its light resets your night vision.

**Look at the whole sky.** Meteors can appear anywhere. They seem to come from the constellation Perseus, in the northeast, but you don't need to find it.

**Be patient.** Meteors come in bursts and lulls. Some nights are better than others.

You can also watch a night or two before or after the peak.

## A quiet wish ritual

Shooting stars have carried wishes for centuries. Here's a simple ritual for a summer night:

1. Before you go out, write down **three wishes**: one for yourself, one for someone you love and one for the world.
2. Lie back and watch the sky. Let your mind quiet.
3. Each time you see a meteor, silently say one wish.
4. Before you go inside, sit for a moment and notice how small and how connected you feel under that sky.

It's less about the wishes coming true and more about remembering what you care about.
