Caring for someone you love can be one of the most meaningful things you ever do. It can also slowly empty you out.

Whether you're looking after an aging parent, a partner who's ill, a child with extra needs or all of the above, you're probably the person everyone else leans on. And when you're the one holding everything up, it's hard to notice you're sinking.

## Signs you may be running on empty

Caregiver burnout often creeps in quietly. You might notice:

- **You're tired in a way sleep doesn't fix.** Waking up already exhausted.
- **You feel resentful, then guilty for feeling resentful.** Both feelings are normal. Neither makes you a bad person.
- **Your world has shrunk.** You've stopped seeing friends, doing hobbies or doing anything just for you.
- **You're short-tempered** with the person you care for, or with everyone else.
- **You've stopped looking after yourself.** Skipped appointments, skipped meals, no time outside.
- **You feel invisible.** Everyone asks how they're doing. Nobody asks how you are.

If several of these sound familiar, it's a sign to make some changes. It's not a sign of failure.

## Why caregivers burn out

It's not because you're weak. It's because caregiving is relentless, often unpaid and usually invisible. There's no clocking out. And many caregivers, especially women, were raised to believe their needs come last.

Running on empty doesn't make you a better caregiver. It just makes everything harder for everyone, including you.

## Small ways to refill

You may not be able to change the situation, but you can protect a little space for yourself inside it.

**Name one need, out loud.** Tell a sibling, partner or friend one specific thing that would help: "Can you take Mom to her Thursday appointment?" Specific asks get better answers than "I need more help."

**Take ten minutes that belong only to you.** Step outside, sit in the car with music, drink a cup of tea while it's still hot. Ten minutes is small enough to actually happen.

**Get some daylight.** Even a short walk around the block gives your mind a break from the indoor world of care.

**Let something be good enough.** The house doesn't have to be clean. Dinner can be cereal. Lower the bar on everything that isn't essential.

**Look for respite options.** Local agencies, adult day programs, faith communities and caregiver support groups exist to give caregivers a break. It's worth one phone call to find out what's near you.

## Talk to someone who's on your side

Caregivers often have nobody to talk to who isn't also involved. A support call gives you 30 minutes with a trained, neutral person who's there for *you*. Not for the person you care for, not for the family. Just you, and how you're really doing.

You matter in this story too.

*If you ever feel you might hurt yourself or the person you care for, please call or text 988 (US) or call 911 right away. Reaching out is the right thing to do.*
