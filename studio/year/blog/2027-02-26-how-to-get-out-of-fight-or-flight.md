"How do I get out of fight-or-flight?"

Your heart is pounding. Your jaw is tight. You're ready to argue with someone, or run out of the room, and nothing has even happened yet. Or something did happen, hours ago, and your body still hasn't gotten the memo.

That's fight-or-flight. And getting out of it is the first thing we help people do.

## What is fight-or-flight?

Fight-or-flight is your body's alarm system. When your brain senses danger, it floods you with energy so you can fight the threat or get away from it. Your heart speeds up. Your breathing gets fast and shallow. Your muscles tense. Your focus narrows.

It's a brilliant system. It's also a system designed for chasing off bears, not reading a passive-aggressive email from your sister-in-law.

The problem isn't that it turns on. The problem is when it doesn't turn off.

## What are the signs I'm stuck in fight-or-flight?

Some common ones:

- Racing heart or a fluttery chest, even when you're sitting still.
- Short, shallow breaths up in your chest.
- Feeling irritable, snappy or on edge.
- Clenched jaw, tight shoulders, fists.
- Trouble sleeping, or waking up already wired.
- Thinking in worst-case scenarios.
- Wanting to fix, argue, leave or control everything right now.

If several of these sound familiar, your body is probably working overtime to keep you safe. That deserves kindness, not a lecture.

## Why can't I calm down even when I know I'm safe?

Because the alarm system doesn't run on logic. It runs on signals.

You can tell yourself, "This is fine," a hundred times. If your breathing is still fast, your muscles are still tight and you're still alone with it, your body keeps hearing "danger."

To get out of fight-or-flight, you have to speak your body's language. Slow breath. Steady ground. A calm presence. Those are signals of safety.

## How do I get out of fight-or-flight mode?

Here are five steps. They're the same ones in our free [10-Minute Human Reset](/free/human-reset), because they work well together.

1. **Name it.** "I'm in fight-or-flight right now." Simply naming it can help you step back a little from the wave.
2. **Breathe out slowly.** In through your nose for four. Out for six to eight. The long exhale is the brake pedal. Do it at least five times.
3. **Look around.** Slowly scan the room. Name five things you see. Let your eyes rest on something pleasant: a plant, a window, a pet.
4. **Feel your feet.** Press them into the floor. Shake out your hands. Roll your shoulders. Give that energy somewhere to go. A brisk walk counts.
5. **Reach for a human.** Call or text someone calm. Not someone who'll pour gasoline on it. Someone steady.

Then wait 20 minutes before you make any big decision or send any big message. Future you will be grateful.

## Why does talking to someone calm help so much?

Because nervous systems are contagious. When you're around someone who's panicking, you tend to panic. When you're around someone grounded, you tend to settle.

## When should I get more help?

If fight-or-flight feels constant, if you're having panic attacks, or if it's affecting your sleep, work or health for weeks, please see your doctor or a licensed therapist. That's real, it's common, and it deserves proper care.
