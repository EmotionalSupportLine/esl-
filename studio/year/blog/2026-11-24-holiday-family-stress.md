The holidays are supposed to be warm. And sometimes they are. But for a lot of us, walking into a family gathering also means walking back into old roles: the peacekeeper, the one who cooks, the one who gets criticized, the one who's "too sensitive."

You can love your family and still find the holidays exhausting. Both are true for a lot of people.

Here's how to protect your energy this year.

## Decide what you're actually willing to give

Before the season starts, take five minutes and ask yourself:

- How many events do I genuinely want to go to?
- How long do I want to stay at each one?
- What am I willing to host, cook or pay for, and what am I not?

Write your answers down. When the invitations and requests start, you'll have something to check against besides guilt.

## Arrive with a plan

**Set your exit time before you go.** "We'll head out around eight" is easier to say at the door than in the middle of dessert.

**Have your own transportation if you can.** Being able to leave is a quiet form of freedom.

**Choose your topics.** If certain subjects always turn into arguments, decide ahead of time that you won't engage. A simple "I'd rather not get into that today" is enough.

**Bring an ally.** A partner, sibling or friend who knows your plan can help steer conversations and notice when you need a break.

## Take small breaks on purpose

You don't have to stay in the busiest room all day.

- Step outside for a few minutes of fresh air and sky
- Offer to take the dog for a walk or run an errand
- Wash dishes in the quiet kitchen
- Take three slow breaths in the bathroom, which is a perfectly good refuge

Small breaks keep your tank from running dry before the evening is over.

## When old roles come back

It's very common to feel like your teenage self around family, even if you're a capable adult everywhere else. When you notice it, try a gentle reminder: *I'm allowed to respond as who I am now.*

You don't have to win the argument, fix your brother or make your mother understand. You only have to look after yourself in the room.

## Afterward, decompress

Plan something restoring for the day after a big gathering: a slow morning, a walk, an early night, a hot bath. And notice what you learned. What drained you? What felt good? Those notes will help you plan next year.
